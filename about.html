Overview

Why hybrid, and why this matters

Collaborative filtering alone can't explain why a restaurant was picked. Content-based filtering alone can't tell you if diners were actually satisfied. Star ratings alone miss what review text really says. This system fuses all three into a single ranking, then learns how to weight them per user — instead of one fixed formula applied to everyone.

🤝

Collaborative Filtering

Recommends based on what similar users rated highly — the "people like you" signal, powered by matrix factorization and neighborhood models.

🍜

Content-Based Filtering

Recommends based on cuisine, price, and location similarity to restaurants a user already liked — grounded in what the restaurant actually is.

💬

Sentiment Analysis

Reads customer review text to gauge real satisfaction beyond the star rating — catching the gap between a 4-star review and a lukewarm paragraph.

Live engine

What actually runs on this site

The Explore page is powered by a working implementation of two of the three arms. Here is exactly what happens when you change a filter.

📐

Step 1 — Content-based arm (TF-IDF + cosine)

Every restaurant is converted into a bag of attribute tokens — cuisines (weighted ×3), ambience, facilities, dietary flags, price band and location. Inverse document frequency is computed across the whole catalogue, so a common token like dine-in counts for far less than a rare one like Chettinad. Your stated preferences become a query vector in the same space, and each restaurant is scored by cosine similarity against it.

🤝

Step 2 — Collaborative arm (item-item)

400 synthetic rater profiles with stable cuisine and budget affinities produce a sparse user×item rating matrix. Item ratings are mean-centred and an item-item cosine similarity matrix is precomputed. When you like or rate a restaurant, a score is predicted for every other restaurant as a similarity-weighted average over the things you reacted to — the approach from Sarwar et al. (2001).

⚖️

Step 3 — Normalise, then blend

The two arms produce scores on different natural scales, so each is min–max normalised to [0, 1] across the candidate set before blending. Otherwise whichever arm happened to have the wider spread would dominate the ranking for reasons that have nothing to do with quality.

🧊

Step 4 — Cold start

A brand-new visitor has rated nothing, so the collaborative arm has no history to work from and returns nothing at all. Rather than emit a meaningless zero, the system substitutes a popularity score (review volume scaled by rating) until real feedback exists. The explanation text changes to say so.

What the live site does not do

The sentiment arm described below is part of the trained research pipeline but is not wired into this front end — the sample listings carry no review text for VADER to score. Data

Datasets & the synthetic fallback layer

Built on Zomato-style and Swiggy-style restaurant catalogues (Kaggle). Since public catalogues don't include per-user rating history, a synthetic layer of ~400 users' ratings and reviews fills that gap — and its limitations are reported honestly, not hidden.

Restaurant catalogues

Zomato-style and Swiggy-style datasets sourced from Kaggle, giving cuisine, price, location, and aggregate rating data across two independently structured sources — used for cross-dataset validation throughout.

Synthetic user layer

~400 synthetic users with ratings and review text, generated to simulate per-user history the public catalogues don't provide. Flagged explicitly wherever it inflates results (see Findings).

Novel contributions

What's actually new here

The hybrid architecture itself follows prior work. These two pieces are the project's original contribution.

1

Personalized fusion weights

Instead of one fixed blend formula applied to every user, each user gets their own weighting across the three signals — learned via Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS) regression on their own rating history. The system adapts to whether a given diner leans on peer opinions, personal taste profile, or review sentiment.

2

Explainability layer

Every recommendation ships with a plain-English reason — e.g. "recommended because similar diners rated it highly" or "matches your usual cuisine and price range" — instead of a black-box score.

Findings

Honest results, not just the good ones

These are worth featuring rather than hiding — they're what make the evaluation credible.

The Popularity baseline sometimes beats smarter models on raw RMSE

A known effect in recommender systems: minimizing rating error isn't the same as producing a good ranked list. Explained and resolved by looking at the ranking metrics (Precision@K, NDCG@K) alongside RMSE, not RMSE alone.

Personalized fusion weights helped on one dataset but not the other

The NNLS-learned weighting improved ranking quality on one of the two datasets and showed no clear gain on the other — reported as-is rather than cherry-picked, and discussed as a direction for further work.

Sentiment classifier scores are inflated on synthetic data

Because the ~400-user layer is synthetically generated, sentiment classification accuracy on it is higher than it would likely be on organic review text — flagged explicitly rather than presented at face value.
